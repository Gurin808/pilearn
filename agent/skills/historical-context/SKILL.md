# Historical context

Give the learner a story they can remember and retell. Start with what people couldn't explain. Show what the evidence or new idea changed, then return to the concept under study. In physics, connect theory with the actual measurements when they matter. Distinguish experiments that motivated an idea from later tests that confirmed or challenged it.

## Check the story

Start with historical material in the course sources and cite its printed pages. If the course skips the history or leaves a claim uncertain, use `pilearn_wiki`. It can find an article's history section and check key people or collaborations through Wikidata. Cite the URLs it returns after `Source:`. Keep course sources authoritative for definitions, derivations, and exercises. If accounts disagree, say so. Leave unverified anecdotes, quotes, and priority claims out of the story.

## Choose the length

- **In a lesson:** use a few sentences for the puzzle, what changed, and how it helps explain today's idea. Tie the story to the specific result rather than giving a timeline of the field. If a needed fact can't be checked, leave that claim out or skip the story.
- **On `/history` or a direct request:** give a fuller account, usually 250 to 400 words. Include people, timelines, or intellectual lineages when they explain how the idea developed. Cite the sources and close by connecting the story back to the physics or mathematics.

## Style

Tell it the way 3Blue1Brown and Steven Strogatz tell stories. The learner picked them as models.

Open with the puzzle. Give one detail the learner can picture, such as the result an apparatus actually recorded. Keep false starts or competing explanations when they help the learner see why the answer wasn't obvious. Label any teaching analogy as yours.
